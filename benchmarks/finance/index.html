Explainable reasoning, on financial reports

It shows its working. Every number points at the page, and every step says how sure it is.

More on this

Nothing on this page is re-scored. Every figure counts these answers as wrong, as the benchmark does, and every published figure was scored against the same key, so the comparisons hold. What changes is what a score on FinQA means: nobody can reach 100%, and that includes the human experts' 87.5% and 91.2%. The first three checks are mechanical and need no judgement. The 30 read by hand are a convenience sample, not a draw from all misses, so no rate is taken from them.

Method and sources

The benchmark. FinQA (Chen et al., EMNLP 2021): 8,281 questions written by finance experts over pages of S&P 500 annual reports, each with the expert's working as a short program. Official split: 6,251 to train on, 883 to develop with, 1,147 to test. Scored by the benchmark's own evaluation code, unchanged. Working exactly right is its program accuracy; final number right is its execution accuracy.

Our models. Both trained on the 6,251 training questions only, one attempt each, 28–29 September 2026. Every choice, including when to stop training, was made on the development set. The test set was run once, afterwards. A second pass that combined the two models did better on the development set and worse on the test set, so it is not used.

What the model outputs. A sequence of steps, each an operation and two inputs. Every piece is picked from what the model is given: the page, the question, a fixed list of operations and constants, and the results of earlier steps. The benchmark's calculator runs the steps.

Confidence. Each piece of the working has the probability the model gave it; the confidence of a whole answer is those multiplied together. The per-step figures in the demo come from the development set, where they were logged; the per-answer figures further down are from the test set. The line in the demo is the development set's least-sure fifth (55%); on the test set that fifth starts at 54%.

Bars. Swept on 27 September 2026 across papers, lab reports and the two live leaderboards. APOLLO: arXiv 2212.07249. TAT-LLM: arXiv 2401.13223. EEDP: arXiv 2402.11194. FINDER: arXiv 2510.13157 (EMNLP 2025). Frontier subset figures: TaNOS, arXiv 2604.21495 (June 2026). Human experts: the FinQA paper.

Price. CiteOnly's figure is what you pay: $7 per 1,000 questions ($5 on the smaller model). It is tentative, and calculated on the documents in this benchmark. Frontier costs are estimates from OpenRouter list prices read on 28 September 2026.

Time-to-check estimate. Built from the test set's measured answers: how many steps and numbers each answer has, and how confidence orders them. The seconds and minutes are assumptions, set by the sliders. The row for written working assumes the same number of steps and numbers as ours, and gives it no credit or blame for accuracy, which is unmeasured here.

The answer key. Two mechanical checks over the key's own fields, run 29 September 2026: its written answer against the result of its own program, to the precision written, allowing percent as a fraction and ignoring sign; and our marked-wrong numbers against the written answer, sign included. Answers such as "yes", or too coarse to tell two sums apart ("8%"), are not checked. Separately, 30 sure answers marked wrong on the development set were read by hand against the page; that reading is ours, and no rate is drawn from it.

The demo. Six development-set questions from the annual reports of Ball Corporation (2010), Union Pacific (2014), Loews (2015), Marathon Oil (2015 and 2007) and Lockheed Martin (2006). Page text, tables and the key's working are FinQA's; the working, the confidences and the runner-ups are the model's stored output for that question.

Scope. One attempt per model, so no spread across repeats. The working score's bars are old because the field stopped reporting it. The scores count every key mistake against us, as they do against everyone.
